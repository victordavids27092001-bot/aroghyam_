# Qualitative Research Skill — Arogyam UX Discover

## Purpose

Use this skill during **Phase 01 — Discover** to understand how people currently make healthcare and wellbeing decisions, what problems they experience, why those problems occur, and what they need from a trustworthy service.

The skill must discover problems from actual qualitative evidence. It must never report an anticipated problem, business assumption, or feature request as a research finding unless participant evidence supports it.

## Product Context

Arogyam is an Indian integrative-wellness application intended to support verified Ayurveda and Siddha care, evidence-based food and nutrition education, supervised diet plans, wellness activities, reviewed traditional remedies, nearby facilities, medicine ordering and delivery, authenticated traditional medicinal products, laboratory services, and consent-based health records.

India is the primary market, with additional attention to Tamil Nadu, Siddha services, Tamil-language access, regional food behaviour, and rural, semi-urban, and urban experiences.

## Research Objective

Discover and explain:

- What users are trying to accomplish
- What they currently do
- Why they behave that way
- Which people, services, tools, and information influence them
- Where they experience difficulty, risk, uncertainty, delay, or loss of trust
- Which workarounds they use
- What outcomes matter to them
- Which needs are unmet
- How context changes across different users and locations
- Which assumptions about Arogyam are supported, contradicted, or still unresolved

## Problems to Investigate, Not Assume

Treat the following as research themes rather than predetermined findings:

### Trust and credibility

- Difficulty verifying practitioners, sellers, medicines, raw products, facilities, or laboratories
- Confusion between traditional use, promotional claims, and clinical evidence
- Reliance on family, community, testimonials, influencers, or brand familiarity
- Concern about online consultations or digital payments

### Access and inclusion

- Rural, semi-urban, and urban service differences
- Travel, waiting time, affordability, language, disability, literacy, device, bandwidth, or payment barriers
- Dependence on caregivers or shared devices
- Difficulty using English medical terminology

### Care discovery and continuity

- Difficulty finding appropriate care
- Fragmented consultation, prescription, pharmacy, laboratory, and follow-up journeys
- Repeating health information across providers
- Lost prescriptions or reports
- Lack of continuity between traditional and conventional care

### Food and nutrition

- Difficulty applying generic diet advice to regional foods, budgets, routines, preferences, or household decisions
- Confusion about food-as-medicine claims
- Tension between practitioner advice and family food practices
- Conditions requiring professional nutrition support

### Traditional remedies and medicine safety

- Unsafe self-medication or delayed conventional care
- Unclear dosage, duration, preparation, contraindications, or interactions
- Concerns about authenticity, contamination, substitution, expiry, or storage
- Difficulty understanding when a remedy is educational information versus treatment advice

### Medicine ordering and delivery

- Prescription, stock, substitution, delivery, storage, returns, and refund problems
- Trust in pharmacies, suppliers, and delivery partners
- Difficulty identifying raw medicinal ingredients or product sources
- Lack of clear support after adverse effects or delivery problems

### Laboratory tests and health records

- Uncertainty about which tests are needed
- Confusion about laboratory reports and reference ranges
- Difficulty storing and sharing reports
- Privacy and consent concerns
- Dependence on a practitioner or caregiver for interpretation

### Safety and escalation

- Failure to recognize symptoms requiring urgent or emergency care
- Overconfidence in natural or traditional products
- Stopping prescribed treatment without appropriate advice
- Difficulty knowing when teleconsultation is insufficient

These themes guide inquiry. Report them as findings only when supported by participant evidence.

## Research-Planning Workflow

### 1. Define the decision

State:

- The product or research decision to support
- What is already known from secondary research
- What remains unknown
- Which assumptions are most important or risky
- Which user groups and contexts are affected
- What qualitative research can and cannot answer

### 2. Form research questions

Use open questions about behaviour, context, meaning, motivation, trust, barriers, and decision-making.

Good research questions include:

- How do people decide whether an Ayurveda or Siddha practitioner is trustworthy?
- How do households convert health or diet advice into everyday meals?
- What happens from receiving a prescription to obtaining and using a medicine?
- How do people store, understand, and share laboratory reports?
- What makes users continue self-care, contact a practitioner, or seek urgent care?

Avoid research questions that assume the solution, such as “Would users like an AI diet-plan feature?”

### 3. Select qualitative methods

Choose methods according to the question:

| Research need | Suitable method | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Motivations, beliefs, trust, and past experiences | In-depth interview | Self-report may differ from real behaviour |
| Current workflow and environmental constraints | Contextual inquiry | Requires careful consent and observation access |
| Behaviour or decisions over time | Diary study | Participant effort can create missing or selective data |
| Household or caregiver roles | Paired or caregiver interview | Power relationships may suppress honest responses |
| Practitioner or partner processes | Stakeholder interview/service walkthrough | Stakeholder claims are not user evidence |
| Shared terminology or group norms | Carefully facilitated focus group | Social pressure can reduce disclosure; avoid for sensitive personal health details |

Use remote or in-person methods according to participant access, safety, privacy, and research objectives.

### 4. Define participants

Segment by relevant behaviour and context rather than demographics alone.

Potential groups include:

- Current Ayurveda or Siddha users
- People considering but not using traditional care
- People managing chronic conditions
- Prevention-focused adults
- Older adults
- Caregivers
- People who order medicines online
- People who purchase traditional raw medicinal products
- Users who book laboratory tests or manage reports
- Rural, semi-urban, and urban users
- Tamil- and English-preference users
- Users with different digital and health-literacy levels
- Users with accessibility needs

Define inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria, behavioural quotas, geographic mix, language mix, accessibility needs, recruitment channel, incentive, and safeguarding requirements.

Use a sample-size range justified by diversity of contexts, research scope, and information quality. Do not promise statistical representation or claim saturation automatically.

### 5. Prepare screening questions

Screeners should verify relevant behaviour without revealing the preferred answer. Avoid collecting sensitive medical data that is not needed for recruitment.

Include:

- Relevant recent behaviour
- Role in healthcare or household decisions
- Geography and access context
- Language preference
- Device and connectivity context
- Caregiver involvement
- Accessibility or participation needs
- Conflicts such as employment by Arogyam or a direct competitor

### 6. Prepare the discussion guide

Organize the guide as:

1. Welcome, consent, recording, and right to stop
2. Participant context
3. Recent real experience
4. Step-by-step current journey
5. Decision points and trust
6. Pain points, risks, emotions, and workarounds
7. Information and people involved
8. Differences between ideal and actual experience
9. Reflection and participant questions
10. Closing, support, and data-use reminder

Ask about recent events before hypothetical opinions.

Prefer:

- “Tell me about the last time…”
- “What happened next?”
- “How did you decide?”
- “What information did you use?”
- “Can you show me how you currently do that?”
- “What made that difficult?”
- “Was there a time when the opposite happened?”

Avoid:

- Leading questions
- Selling Arogyam
- Medical judgement
- Asking multiple questions at once
- Suggesting that traditional or conventional care is inherently better
- Asking users to disclose unnecessary diagnoses or sensitive information
- Treating stated future intent as observed behaviour

## Healthcare Research Safeguards

- Obtain informed consent before research and separate consent for recording.
- Explain purpose, data use, storage, access, retention, and withdrawal.
- Minimize collection of personal and health information.
- Do not diagnose, prescribe, interpret reports, or recommend treatment during research.
- Prepare a protocol for distress, disclosures of harm, emergency statements, adverse events, and requests for medical advice.
- Do not recruit in ways that create pressure from practitioners, employers, or caregivers.
- Provide private participation options when household or caregiver presence could influence responses.
- Use qualified interpreters or researchers for Tamil sessions; do not rely on unreviewed machine translation for sensitive content.
- Remove direct identifiers from research outputs unless explicit authorized handling is required.
- Obtain relevant ethics, legal, privacy, or institutional review when the study requires it.

## Conducting Sessions

The moderator must:

- Build rapport without promising benefit
- Remain neutral
- Follow the participant’s language where possible
- Probe behaviour, context, causes, and consequences
- Observe contradictions without accusing the participant
- Separate facilitator notes from participant quotations
- Record assistance, interruptions, caregiver influence, and environmental conditions
- Avoid completing the participant’s story
- Allow silence and reflection
- End safely and explain next steps

## Evidence Capture

For every session, retain a traceable record containing:

- Participant code, not public identity
- Relevant segment and context
- Method, date, mode, and language
- Consent and recording status
- Observation or quotation
- Researcher note or interpretation kept separately
- Journey stage
- Theme code
- Confidence and limitations
- Safety or privacy concern requiring escalation

Do not fabricate quotations or rewrite participant meaning to sound more polished.

## Analysis Workflow

1. Clean and organize notes while preserving source traceability.
2. Separate observed behaviour, participant statement, and researcher interpretation.
3. Code data inductively and against research questions.
4. Group related evidence using affinity mapping.
5. Identify themes, needs, pain points, workarounds, decision factors, and emotions.
6. Look for contradictions, outliers, negative cases, and segment differences.
7. Map important evidence to the current journey and service ecosystem.
8. Assess evidence strength using number of supporting sessions, context diversity, specificity, and contradictory evidence—not frequency alone.
9. Convert findings into problem and need statements.
10. Identify questions requiring more research rather than forcing a conclusion.

## Finding Structure

Write each finding as:

### Finding

A concise statement grounded in behaviour or experience.

### Evidence

- Participant codes
- Relevant quotations or observations
- Contexts represented
- Contradictory evidence

### Why it matters

Effect on the user, care journey, trust, safety, accessibility, or service delivery.

### Limitation

What the evidence does not establish and which populations were not represented.

### UX implication

A design or research consideration written as a hypothesis, not a final solution.

### Next question

What still needs to be tested, observed, or quantified.

## Problem and Need Statements

Use these formats:

> **Problem:** [User/context] experiences [observable problem] when [situation] because [evidence-backed cause], leading to [consequence].

> **Need:** [User/context] needs a way to [need independent of a feature] so that [desired outcome].

Avoid embedding solutions such as apps, dashboards, AI, notifications, or subscriptions inside the need statement.

## Confidence Labels

- **Strong qualitative support:** Clear pattern across relevant and varied contexts with little material contradiction
- **Moderate qualitative support:** Repeated evidence, but limited contexts or meaningful contradictions remain
- **Early signal:** Important evidence from few or narrow contexts requiring more research
- **Contradictory:** Different participants or contexts show materially different patterns
- **Assumption only:** No participant evidence yet

These labels do not measure population prevalence.

## Research Integrity Rules

- Never invent participants, sessions, recordings, quotations, observations, themes, or findings.
- Clearly label proposed research separately from completed research.
- Do not claim that a problem exists merely because it appears in the BRD or secondary research.
- Do not quantify prevalence from a qualitative sample.
- Do not claim statistical representation.
- Do not claim saturation without documenting how it was assessed.
- Do not hide contradictory evidence.
- Do not combine distinct participant groups into one false average.
- Do not treat stakeholder opinions as user findings.
- Do not convert a requested feature directly into a user need.
- Do not recommend diagnosis or treatment.
- Preserve participant dignity and cultural context.

## Quality Gate

The qualitative-research work is ready for Define only when:

- Research questions are connected to important product decisions.
- Recruitment covers relevant behaviours and contexts or gaps are disclosed.
- Consent, privacy, inclusion, language, and safeguarding are addressed.
- Findings are traceable to participant evidence.
- Observations, interpretations, assumptions, and recommendations are distinguishable.
- Contradictions and negative cases are included.
- UX problems describe causes and consequences rather than only feature requests.
- Limitations are explicit.
- No final interface or MVP has been selected prematurely.
