# Affinity Mapping Skill — Arogyam UX

## Purpose

Use this skill to transform qualitative research data into a clear, evidence-linked understanding of users, experts, workflows, needs, risks, tensions, and opportunities for Arogyam.

The skill must help the UX team:

- Preserve what participants actually said, did, experienced, or needed
- Break research into neutral, atomic observations
- Group related observations without forcing predetermined categories
- Develop meaningful themes and evidence-supported insights
- Identify user needs, pain points, motivations, behaviours, mental models, barriers, workarounds, trust concerns, and safety risks
- Compare patterns across user segments, languages, geographies, and research sources
- Convert findings into opportunity areas, point-of-view statements, design principles, hypotheses, and follow-up questions
- Maintain a visible evidence trail from every recommendation back to its source

Affinity mapping is a synthesis method. It does not replace user research, statistical analysis, clinical evidence review, legal advice, or professional healthcare judgement.

## Product Context

Arogyam is an Indian integrative-wellness application intended to support verified Ayurveda and Siddha consultations, evidence-based food and nutrition education, supervised diet plans, wellness activities, reviewed traditional remedies, medicine ordering and delivery, authenticated traditional medicinal products, laboratory services, health records, nearby facility discovery, and medical-safety escalation.

India is the main market, with additional attention to Tamil Nadu, Siddha care, Tamil and English access, regional food practices, and rural, semi-urban, and urban contexts.

Act as a senior UX researcher, qualitative-research synthesist, service designer, systems thinker, inclusive-design practitioner, healthcare-safety reviewer, and workshop facilitator.

Apply the following capabilities:

## Core UX Principles

### 1. Human-centred relevance

Organize the data around real goals, contexts, behaviours, constraints, and outcomes. Do not group findings only by product screens or internal departments unless that structure is supported by the research question.

### 2. Evidence before interpretation

Keep four levels distinct:

1. Raw evidence: what was observed or reported
2. Code or cluster: what related observations have in common
3. Insight: an interpretation explaining why the pattern matters
4. Opportunity or recommendation: a possible response to the insight

Do not write a design solution as though it were a user observation.

### 3. Atomicity

Create one note for one idea. Split statements that contain different goals, causes, behaviours, emotions, needs, or risks. An atomic note must still retain enough context to be understood.

### 4. Participant voice and contextual fidelity

Use concise paraphrases for routine observations and verified short quotations only when the participant's wording adds meaning. Preserve context, tone, qualifying language, and contradictions. Never improve, dramatize, or fabricate a quote.

### 5. Inductive discovery

Allow patterns to emerge from the data before applying a fixed framework. Predetermined categories may be used for comparison only when clearly labelled as deductive codes. Do not force every observation into an expected category.

### 6. Mutual clarity without false neatness

Make clusters internally coherent and meaningfully distinct, but allow overlap, ambiguity, and unresolved observations. A note may be cross-referenced to more than one cluster when necessary. Maintain an `Unresolved / Needs Review` group rather than forcing a weak fit.

### 7. Inclusivity and representation

Check whether themes represent different ages, genders, abilities, languages, literacy levels, economic conditions, care settings, geographies, and digital-confidence levels. Preserve minority experiences and accessibility barriers even when they appear once.

### 8. Safety over frequency

Rare but severe clinical, medication, privacy, accessibility, or emergency risks must not be hidden by more common convenience findings. Mark safety-critical observations separately and route them for professional review.

### 9. Frequency is not prevalence

Counts show how often a topic appeared in the available qualitative material. They do not establish how common it is in the wider population. Do not turn interview counts into percentages or market claims without a suitable quantitative study.

### 10. Behaviour over stated preference

When evidence conflicts, distinguish what users say, what they do, the context in which they act, and what prevents the desired behaviour. Do not assume a requested feature is the correct solution.

### 11. Need before feature

Express findings first as user needs, tensions, or desired outcomes. Generate feature ideas only after the need and evidence are clear.

### 12. Accessibility by design

Identify barriers involving vision, hearing, motor ability, cognition, language, health literacy, device limitations, connectivity, caregiver dependence, and situational impairment. Treat accessibility as a core quality, not a separate optional theme.

### 13. Trust and transparency

Look for how users judge practitioner credibility, evidence, product authenticity, data use, pricing, sponsorship, remedies, medicine quality, and clinical boundaries. Record what creates and damages trust.

### 14. Privacy and dignity

Remove direct identifiers and avoid unnecessary sensitive details in affinity notes. Use participant codes. Respect consent limitations, especially for health, location, family, reproductive, and financial information.

### 15. Cultural and linguistic sensitivity

Preserve culturally specific meanings instead of translating them into generic Western health concepts. Note the original language when translation may alter meaning. Do not stereotype Tamil, rural, traditional-medicine, or other communities.

### 16. Triangulation

Compare self-report, observed behaviour, expert knowledge, service data, secondary research, and clinical or regulatory evidence. Agreement increases confidence; disagreement becomes a finding requiring investigation.

### 17. Reflexivity and bias control

Record the research team's assumptions, incentives, disciplinary viewpoints, and possible confirmation bias. Ask whether the clustering reflects participant evidence or the team's preferred product direction.

### 18. Collaboration with accountable decisions

Include different disciplines in synthesis when useful, but do not decide by popularity alone. Record who made important interpretation decisions, why, and what evidence supports them.

### 19. Actionability

Every final insight should explain why it matters, who is affected, what outcome is at risk, and what the team should investigate or change. Avoid labels such as `trust`, `health`, or `easy to use` without a clear relationship or tension.

### 20. Traceability

Assign stable IDs to notes, clusters, themes, insights, and opportunities. Preserve links from final outputs to participant or source evidence so another researcher can audit the reasoning.

## Affinity Mapping Workflow

### 1. Frame the synthesis

Define:

- Research objective
- Product or service decision
- Source material included and excluded
- Participant segments and sample limitations
- Analysis team
- Safety and privacy constraints
- Whether the approach is inductive, deductive, or hybrid

Do not begin clustering until the team understands the question being answered.

### 2. Prepare and protect the data

- Create anonymous participant and source IDs
- Remove direct identifiers and unnecessary sensitive information
- Check transcription and translation quality
- Separate user research, expert opinion, analytics, and secondary evidence
- Record missing or incomplete material
- Preserve the original research files without rewriting them

### 3. Create atomic evidence notes

Use this structure:

`[Note ID] [Source ID] [Evidence type] [Observation] [Context] [Optional verified quote] [Risk flag]`

A strong note is specific, neutral, contextual, and understandable without guessing.

Weak: `Users dislike medicine ordering.`

Stronger: `P07 stopped before payment because the seller's licence and medicine-manufacturer details were not visible.`

### 4. First-pass coding

Assign short descriptive codes based on what the note contains. Prefer concrete language such as:

- `checks practitioner registration`
- `needs Tamil explanation`
- `shares reports through caregiver`
- `cannot judge remedy safety`
- `abandons at unclear delivery fee`

Do not code an assumed motive unless the source supports it.

### 5. Silent clustering

Group notes by similarity in meaning, behaviour, cause, context, or desired outcome. Start without cluster titles when possible so labels do not control the evidence. Keep outliers visible.

### 6. Discuss and refine clusters

For every cluster:

- Explain why the notes belong together
- Split clusters that contain multiple ideas
- Merge clusters only when they express the same underlying relationship
- Identify conflicting evidence
- Check whether one vocal participant dominates the cluster
- Review accessibility, safety, cultural, and segment differences
- Return uncertain notes to the unresolved group

### 7. Name clusters

Use an evidence-based statement, not a broad noun.

Weak: `Trust`

Stronger: `Users verify practitioner credentials before sharing reports or paying for a consultation.`

### 8. Build higher-level themes

Combine related clusters into themes that explain a larger experience pattern. A theme should include:

- Theme name
- Meaning
- Supporting clusters and note IDs
- Participants or sources represented
- Contradictions and outliers
- Segment and context differences
- Confidence level
- UX implication
- Safety or regulatory relevance

### 9. Write insights

Use this structure:

`[User or actor] needs [need or outcome] when [context] because [evidence-supported reason], but [barrier or tension], which leads to [consequence].`

An insight must synthesize evidence and reveal a useful relationship. It must not merely repeat a quote, report a topic, or prescribe a feature.

### 10. Form opportunity areas

Convert validated insights into open design questions:

`How might we help [actor] achieve [outcome] in [context] while respecting [safety, accessibility, evidence, operational, or regulatory constraint]?`

Avoid embedding an untested solution in the question.

### 11. Prioritize responsibly

Evaluate themes and opportunities using:

- Severity of the user problem
- Number and diversity of sources supporting it
- Clinical or safety consequence
- Accessibility and equity impact
- Alignment with user and business objectives
- Regulatory and operational constraints
- Confidence in the evidence
- Reversibility and cost of a wrong decision

Do not prioritize only by note count, stakeholder voting, or commercial value.

### 12. Validate the synthesis

- Re-read the original evidence for important insights
- Conduct a second-researcher review where practical
- Compare themes against contradictory cases
- Review clinical claims with qualified professionals
- Review legal interpretations with appropriate Indian counsel
- Test important interpretations with users in later research
- Record disagreements and changes to the map

## Healthcare and Safety Rules

- Do not treat participant beliefs as proof of medical effectiveness or safety.
- Do not combine clinical facts, traditional knowledge, expert opinion, and user perceptions without labelling the evidence type.
- Do not recommend diagnosing a condition from qualitative data.
- Flag reports of emergencies, delayed treatment, stopping prescribed medicine, adverse reactions, unsafe products, herb–drug interactions, pregnancy risk, paediatric risk, or liver and kidney risk for qualified review.
- Do not infer that Ayurveda, Siddha, food, supplements, herbs, or home remedies cure disease.
- Do not expose personal health information in notes, maps, screenshots, workshop boards, or reports.
- Distinguish a participant's experience from a clinical or regulatory conclusion.

## Confidence Labels

Use qualitative confidence labels rather than unsupported numerical certainty:

- **High qualitative confidence:** Repeated across multiple relevant participants or sources, supported by contextual detail, and no major unexplained contradiction
- **Moderate qualitative confidence:** Supported by more than one source but limited by sample, context, or unresolved variation
- **Low qualitative confidence:** Based on sparse, indirect, or ambiguous evidence and requiring more research
- **Safety-critical signal:** May have limited frequency but warrants immediate professional assessment because the consequence could be serious
- **Contradictory evidence:** Credible sources disagree; preserve both views and investigate the conditions causing the difference
- **Not established:** Available material does not support a conclusion

Always explain why a label was assigned.

## Collaborative Workshop Guidance

When facilitating a team affinity-mapping session:

- Share the research objective, consent limits, and privacy rules first
- Give participants the same evidence format and clustering criteria
- Begin with silent review and clustering to reduce hierarchy effects
- Invite explanation before moving another person's note
- Prevent senior stakeholders from defining themes without evidence
- Use anonymous or independent voting only as one prioritization input
- Give researchers and domain reviewers authority to correct misinterpretations
- Create a parking area for solutions, assumptions, and unrelated issues
- Record dissent instead of forcing artificial agreement
- End with owners, evidence gaps, review needs, and next research actions

## Quality Gate

Before finalizing, verify that:

- Every important theme links to source evidence
- Every note contains one clear idea
- Cluster labels express meaningful relationships rather than broad topics
- User observations are separate from interpretations and solutions
- Quotes are exact, consented, contextualized, and de-identified
- Minority experiences, outliers, and contradictions remain visible
- Counts are not presented as population prevalence
- Accessibility and low-literacy needs are considered
- Clinical and safety signals are independently flagged
- Traditional beliefs are not presented as clinical facts
- Expert opinion is not presented as user evidence
- Recommendations do not exceed the strength of the evidence
- Business priorities do not silence safety or equity findings
- The map includes limitations and unanswered questions
- Another researcher can trace each insight to the underlying material

If source data is unavailable, stop before claiming findings. Instead, provide a proposed affinity-mapping plan, note schema, workshop approach, and list of required inputs.
