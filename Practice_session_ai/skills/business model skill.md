# Business Model Skill — Arogyam Offline-to-Online Wellness Community

## Purpose

Use this skill to create a sustainable and responsible business model for Arogyam. The central business challenge is:

> How might Arogyam help people who currently discover, access, and manage wellness services offline become confident, active members of an online health and wellbeing community—while preserving human trust, professional care, cultural relevance, privacy, accessibility, and continuity with local services?

The skill must connect user value, clinical safety, service operations, partner value, community value, revenue, cost, evidence, and measurable outcomes. Moving customers online must mean improved continuity and access—not removing offline choices or forcing users into a digital channel.

## Product Context

Arogyam is an Indian integrative-wellness application supporting:

- Verified Ayurveda and Siddha practitioners
- Online and in-person consultations
- Evidence-based food and nutrition education
- Practitioner-supervised diet plans
- Yoga, breathing, sleep, stress-management, and wellness activities
- Evidence-reviewed traditional and home-remedy information
- Medicine ordering and delivery
- Authenticated traditional medicinal and raw medicinal products
- Laboratory booking and report management
- Consent-based health records
- Nearby hospital, clinic, pharmacy, and laboratory discovery
- Emergency and conventional-care escalation
- A moderated wellness community

India is the main market, with additional attention to Tamil Nadu, Siddha care, Tamil and English access, regional food practices, rural and semi-urban access, assisted digital use, and variable health and digital literacy.

## Core Business Concept

Build a connected model in which offline trust becomes online continuity:

1. **Discover offline:** Users encounter Arogyam through verified practitioners, clinics, Siddha or Ayurveda facilities, pharmacies, laboratories, community events, employers, local organizations, or trusted referrals.
2. **Join with assistance:** Staff, practitioners, caregivers, or community ambassadors help users understand the value, consent, privacy, and first steps.
3. **Receive first value:** The user completes one meaningful task, such as viewing a care plan, attending a follow-up, finding a facility, accessing a report, or joining a suitable education pathway.
4. **Build confidence:** Clear credentials, evidence labels, transparent pricing, Tamil and English support, responsive help, and safe online–offline handoffs establish trust.
5. **Participate safely:** Users learn, practice, ask questions, and support one another within a professionally governed community.
6. **Continue care:** The platform connects education, appointments, food guidance, wellness activities, medicines, tests, reports, and local care without replacing necessary in-person services.
7. **Create sustainable value:** Ethical subscriptions, service fees, partnerships, and organizational programmes fund the service without driving unnecessary consultations, tests, medicines, supplements, or engagement.

## Effective UX and Business Principles

### 1. Start with a real user problem

Define the unmet need, current behaviour, context, alternatives, and consequence before proposing a channel, feature, community, or revenue model.

### 2. Value before migration

Give users a clear reason to adopt the online service. Do not treat app installation, registration, or data capture as user value.

### 3. Preserve channel choice

Allow users to move between online and offline care. Do not punish people who need phone, caregiver, clinic, or assisted support.

### 4. Trust is part of the business model

Practitioner verification, product authenticity, evidence transparency, privacy, predictable service, clear pricing, and responsive support are value-producing capabilities, not compliance decorations.

### 5. Human-centred onboarding

Minimize required data, explain why it is needed, provide assisted onboarding, and help the user reach first value quickly. Do not demand a complete health profile before it is necessary.

### 6. Progressive disclosure

Introduce advanced features, permissions, community functions, and paid services as they become relevant. Keep safety, price, sponsorship, and consent information visible.

### 7. Recognition over recall

Use familiar language, visible status, next-step guidance, and continuity from offline documents or conversations. Avoid requiring users to memorize clinical or technical terminology.

### 8. Accessibility and inclusion

Design for disability, age, literacy, language, low-end devices, limited storage, weak connectivity, shared phones, assisted use, and different payment access.

### 9. Cultural and linguistic relevance

Support Tamil and English appropriately, reflect regional food and care practices, and avoid stereotypes, tokenism, mystical imagery, or literal translations that alter health meaning.

### 10. Safety before engagement

Do not optimize retention, streaks, notifications, community activity, or revenue in ways that encourage self-treatment, delayed care, anxiety, unnecessary testing, or overuse of products and services.

### 11. User control and informed choice

Make membership, subscriptions, data sharing, reminders, community participation, and marketing optional, understandable, and easy to change or stop.

### 12. Privacy by design

Collect only necessary data for a stated purpose. Separate care, community, analytics, and marketing permissions. Protect health, identity, location, family, and payment information.

### 13. Community is not content distribution

A community needs shared purpose, participation roles, belonging, norms, moderation, professional boundaries, conflict handling, and member value. A feed alone is not a community.

### 14. Design for psychological safety

Members must be able to ask basic questions, disagree respectfully, report harm, control visibility, and participate without shame, stigma, harassment, or pressure to disclose health information.

### 15. Separate peer support from medical advice

Clearly distinguish lived experience, education, professional guidance, prescription, and emergency advice. Prevent community popularity from being interpreted as medical authority.

### 16. Ethical behavioural design

Use reminders, defaults, social proof, and progress feedback to support user goals—not to create compulsion, fear, false urgency, or hidden commercial pressure.

### 17. Transparent value exchange

Users should understand what is free, what is paid, why a fee exists, what data is used, and whether a recommendation is sponsored or commission-linked.

### 18. Align incentives with wellbeing

Revenue must not depend on unnecessary tests, supplements, medicines, consultations, or exaggerated claims. Reward quality, continuity, safe outcomes, and service reliability.

### 19. Design the whole service

Model staff, partners, policies, inventory, verification, fulfilment, delivery, support, escalation, moderation, and record handling—not only the interface.

### 20. Evidence before scale

Validate desirability, comprehension, safety, feasibility, viability, and equity in a limited setting before expanding features, languages, partners, or geography.

### 21. Measure outcomes, not vanity

Do not confuse downloads, impressions, time spent, messages, or group size with meaningful value. Measure task success, informed participation, continuity, trust, safety, retention, and sustainable economics.

### 22. Design for recovery

Provide clear support for failed payments, missed appointments, cancelled tests, delayed deliveries, incorrect records, harmful posts, privacy concerns, adverse events, and loss of connectivity.

## Business Model Design Workflow

### 1. Define the business problem

State:

- Current offline user behaviour
- Unmet need and consequence
- Target segment and context
- Why an online relationship could create additional value
- Why users may resist or be unable to migrate
- Business objective and ethical boundaries
- Geographic and operational scope
- Evidence and assumptions

Do not define success simply as `move users online`.

### 2. Segment by behaviour and need

Consider:

- Prevention-focused adults
- People managing chronic conditions
- Older adults and caregivers
- Women coordinating family wellbeing
- Rural and semi-urban users
- Existing Ayurveda or Siddha users
- People interested in remedies but uncertain about safety
- Low-literacy or low-digital-confidence users
- Offline-only, assisted-digital, mixed-channel, and digital-first users

Avoid segments based only on age or demographics. Include needs, trust, barriers, channel behaviour, and value sought.

### 3. Map the current offline journey

Document:

- Trigger and source of trust
- Discovery and selection
- Travel, waiting, cost, payment, and documentation
- Consultation or service experience
- Medicine, laboratory, or referral handoffs
- Follow-up and support
- Informal family and community influence
- Breakdowns, workarounds, and lost continuity

Identify what users value offline and must not lose online.

### 4. Define the value proposition

For each segment specify:

- Job or desired outcome
- Current alternative
- Pain or risk
- Proposed value
- Proof and trust mechanism
- Reason to join
- Reason to return
- Reason to participate in the community
- Reason to pay, if applicable
- Reason to continue using offline services

Do not lead with technology or a feature inventory.

### 5. Design the offline-to-online bridge

Potential entry points include:

- Practitioner or clinic invitation
- QR code or short link on a verified care summary
- Assisted registration at clinics, pharmacies, laboratories, or events
- Consent-based transfer of appointments, plans, prescriptions, and reports
- Follow-up reminders linked to completed offline care
- Telephone or caregiver-assisted activation
- Tamil and English onboarding
- Community education sessions with digital follow-through
- Employer, association, or local-organization programmes

For each entry point define ownership, consent, support, cost, failure recovery, and measurement.

### 6. Design the activation journey

Activation is the first meaningful user outcome, not account creation. Map:

- Invitation received
- Value and source understood
- Identity and professional relationship verified where necessary
- Minimum consent completed
- First task selected
- First value achieved
- Next useful action offered
- Help and offline alternative visible

Remove unnecessary profile questions and permissions from initial entry.

### 7. Design the community model

Define:

- Shared purpose and member promise
- Eligibility and identity options
- Member roles and contribution paths
- Professional participation boundaries
- Community spaces and topic structure
- Onboarding and norms
- Evidence labels and health-claim rules
- Moderation roles and response times
- Misinformation, harassment, self-harm, emergency, advertising, and adverse-event escalation
- Reporting, appeal, correction, and removal processes
- Recognition that rewards helpfulness without creating fake medical authority
- Privacy, visibility, searchability, and deletion controls
- Measures of member value and safety

Do not launch health-community interaction without governance and trained moderation.

### 8. Map the value network

Include:

- Users and caregivers
- Ayurveda, Siddha, conventional-care, and nutrition professionals
- Clinics, hospitals, pharmacies, and laboratories
- Medicine suppliers and delivery partners
- Community moderators and customer support
- Employers and local organizations
- Technology, identity, payment, location, and health-record partners
- Clinical-safety, evidence, privacy, legal, and regulatory reviewers

For each actor define contribution, received value, responsibilities, incentives, data access, risk, and dependency.

### 9. Design revenue options

Evaluate without assuming they will work:

- Transparent consultation commissions or platform fees
- Optional consumer subscriptions for added continuity or services
- Employer or organizational wellness programmes
- Clinic software and workflow partnerships
- Laboratory booking partnerships
- Medicine fulfilment and delivery fees where lawful and appropriate
- Professional education or operational tools
- Carefully governed sponsored education, clearly labelled and separated from clinical recommendations

Reject or redesign models that reward unnecessary care, product sales, fear, misinformation, hidden advertising, or misuse of health data.

### 10. Map costs and capabilities

Include:

- Product and engineering
- Practitioner verification and re-verification
- Clinical governance and evidence review
- Content creation, translation, and expiry review
- Community moderation and escalation
- Customer support and assisted onboarding
- Pharmacy, laboratory, logistics, and facility operations
- Privacy, security, compliance, legal review, and audit
- Partner onboarding and quality monitoring
- Refunds, disputes, adverse events, and incident response
- Research, accessibility testing, and measurement

### 11. Test unit economics carefully

Where verified data exists, analyse:

- Customer-acquisition cost by channel
- Assisted-onboarding cost
- Activation and repeat-use rates
- Cost to serve by segment and channel
- Contribution margin by service
- Partner payouts and payment costs
- Support, moderation, verification, clinical-governance, refund, and delivery costs
- Retention and lifetime value with clearly stated assumptions
- Subsidy or cross-subsidy requirements

Never invent inputs or hide safety and support costs to make the model appear viable.

### 12. Prioritize assumptions

Rank assumptions by:

- Importance to user value or viability
- Uncertainty
- Safety and privacy consequence
- Cost and reversibility of being wrong
- Evidence needed

Test high-risk assumptions before building a large feature or partnership.

### 13. Run ethical experiments

Use interviews, concierge pilots, landing-page comprehension tests, assisted-onboarding pilots, service prototypes, community cohorts, pricing research, partner pilots, and limited geographic launches.

For each experiment define:

- Hypothesis
- Target segment and sample
- Intervention and comparator where possible
- Success and guardrail metrics
- Consent and privacy controls
- Safety and escalation process
- Stop conditions
- Decision rule

Do not test unsafe medical claims, deceptive scarcity, coerced consent, or intentional denial of necessary care.

### 14. Build the launch model

Choose a focused starting geography and service combination based on verified need, supply, partnerships, language capability, operational readiness, and safety governance. Consider a Tamil Nadu pilot if the evidence and partner readiness support it; do not assume this choice is automatically correct.

### 15. Review and adapt

Track assumptions, results, incidents, complaints, partner quality, community health, unit economics, and equity. Update or stop services that fail safety, value, trust, or viability thresholds.

## Offline-to-Online Funnel

Use these stages without treating them as a purely linear marketing funnel:

1. **Awareness:** User encounters Arogyam through a trusted offline context.
2. **Comprehension:** User understands the service, limits, cost, privacy, and value.
3. **Consent:** User freely chooses online participation and specific data uses.
4. **Assisted entry:** User receives help appropriate to language and digital ability.
5. **Activation:** User achieves the first meaningful health-service outcome.
6. **Trust confirmation:** The promised service is accurate, safe, and dependable.
7. **Continued use:** User returns for a genuine need or ongoing care.
8. **Community participation:** User consumes, contributes, asks, or supports safely.
9. **Online–offline continuity:** Digital activity improves real-world care and follow-up.
10. **Advocacy:** Satisfied users recommend the service without incentives that encourage spam or false claims.

At every stage document barriers, emotional state, channel, support, evidence, safety risks, metric, and recovery path.

## Community Safety and Governance

- Publish plain-language participation rules
- Distinguish verified professionals from community members
- Label professional roles and conflicts of interest
- Prevent diagnosis or prescribing by unqualified users
- Do not allow testimonials to become proof of effectiveness
- Review health claims against authoritative evidence
- Provide reporting for misinformation, adverse events, fraud, harassment, privacy violations, and dangerous advice
- Define urgent escalation for emergency symptoms and threats of harm
- Protect children and vulnerable users with appropriate restrictions
- Prohibit sale or promotion of illegal, adulterated, unverified, or unsafe products
- Separate sponsored content from education and care recommendations
- Record moderation decisions and offer a correction or appeal process
- Train moderators in health misinformation, cultural sensitivity, privacy, and escalation
- Set content review dates and remove outdated material
- Do not expose a member's health condition, location, consultation, purchase, or report activity without explicit permission

## Ethical Revenue Guardrails

Do not recommend:

- Commissions that encourage unnecessary tests, consultations, medicines, supplements, or raw medicinal products
- Ranking practitioners or products mainly by payment
- Selling or repurposing health or location data for advertising without a lawful, explicit, and appropriate basis
- Dark patterns, hidden renewals, forced continuity, difficult cancellation, or misleading free trials
- Sponsored health claims presented as independent evidence
- Gamification that rewards unsafe disclosure, self-treatment, or excessive engagement
- Fear-based upselling or manufactured urgency
- Paywalls that obstruct emergency guidance, critical safety information, consent controls, or access to personal records
- Community incentives that reward popularity as medical credibility

## Measurement Framework

### User-value measures

- Invitation comprehension
- Assisted-onboarding completion
- Time to first meaningful value
- Core task success and error recovery
- Online–offline continuity completion
- Repeat use for a demonstrated need
- User-reported usefulness and trust
- Accessibility and Tamil/English comprehension

### Community measures

- Meaningful participation by segment
- Questions receiving safe and useful responses
- New-member retention
- Helpful contribution rate
- Misinformation reports and correction time
- Harassment, privacy, and safety incidents
- Moderator response and escalation performance
- Member understanding of peer versus professional guidance

### Business measures

- Acquisition and activation by channel
- Cost per activated user
- Assisted-service cost
- Conversion to optional paid value
- Retention and contribution margin
- Partner service quality
- Refund, cancellation, complaint, and support rates
- Revenue concentration and conflict-of-interest exposure

### Safety and equity guardrails

- Delayed-care or unsafe-advice reports
- Adverse events
- Unnecessary-service concerns
- Emergency referral performance
- Privacy and consent incidents
- Outcomes by age, language, geography, disability, literacy, device, and assisted-use status
- Exclusion or abandonment attributable to price or channel design

Do not claim improved health outcomes unless the study design supports that conclusion.

## Quality Gate

Before finalizing, verify that:

- Offline-to-online migration produces clear user value beyond registration
- Offline access and assisted pathways remain available where users need them
- The model preserves trusted offline relationships and enables continuity
- Segments are based on behaviour and needs, not stereotypes
- Community purpose, roles, norms, moderation, escalation, and privacy are defined
- Peer experience is separated from professional medical advice
- Health claims and market claims are evidence-linked
- Practitioner, laboratory, pharmacy, product, and facility verification are addressed
- Revenue incentives do not encourage unnecessary care or products
- Sponsored content and commercial relationships are transparent
- Consent is specific and separated across care, community, analytics, and marketing
- Tamil, accessibility, literacy, device, connectivity, shared-device, and caregiver needs are considered
- Support, moderation, clinical governance, compliance, refunds, and incident costs are included
- Assumptions are not presented as verified demand or financial results
- Activation means first meaningful value, not account creation
- Measures include trust, safety, equity, continuity, and user outcomes—not only engagement
- Experiments include guardrail metrics and stop conditions
- The launch scope matches operational and governance readiness
- Another researcher can trace recommendations to evidence

If evidence is insufficient, present a provisional business model, prioritized assumptions, and research plan rather than claiming market fit or commercial viability.
