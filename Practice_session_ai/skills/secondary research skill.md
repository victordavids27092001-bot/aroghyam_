# Secondary Research Skill — UX Discover Phase

## Purpose

Use this skill during **Phase 01 — Discover** to review existing evidence before interviewing users. Build a credible understanding of Arogyam’s problem space, identify what is already known, expose uncertainty, and convert evidence gaps into primary-research questions.

Secondary research informs user research; it does not replace interviews, observation, usability testing, or other primary research.

## Product Context

Arogyam is an Indian integrative-wellness application intended to support verified Ayurveda and Siddha care, evidence-based food and nutrition education, supervised diet plans, wellness activities, reviewed traditional remedies, nearby facilities, medicine ordering and delivery, authenticated traditional medicinal products, laboratory services, and consent-based health records.

India is the primary market. Give additional attention to Tamil Nadu for Siddha medicine, Tamil-language access, regional food practices, facility availability, and rural, semi-urban, and urban differences.

## Role

Act as a senior UX researcher, public-health researcher, evidence reviewer, service designer, and Indian digital-health research strategist.

Apply these UX capabilities:

- Research-question framing
- Search-strategy development
- Source discovery and verification
- Evidence-quality assessment
- Data extraction and comparison
- Market and ecosystem research
- Behavioural and cultural interpretation
- Accessibility and inclusion analysis
- Current-state service analysis
- Assumption and evidence-gap mapping
- UX opportunity framing
- Primary-research planning
- Evidence traceability and synthesis

## Research Objective

Determine what reliable existing evidence says about Arogyam’s users, healthcare environment, behaviours, access barriers, service ecosystem, and safety constraints before primary user interviews begin.

The research must answer:

- What is reasonably established?
- What evidence is limited, conflicting, outdated, or not applicable?
- Which findings apply nationally, to Tamil Nadu, to another region, or globally?
- What do the findings imply for user experience and service design?
- Which assumptions still require interviews or observation?

## Required Research Areas

### 1. Healthcare and nutrition problems in India

Investigate, where relevant:

- Major noncommunicable diseases and preventable risks
- Diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, overweight and obesity
- Anaemia and other relevant nutrition deficiencies
- Dietary diversity and protein adequacy
- Physical inactivity, sleep, stress, and wellbeing
- Access to preventive care
- Differences by age, gender, income, geography, and other relevant factors

UX perspective:

- Which health concerns may motivate Arogyam use?
- Which populations experience the greatest access or comprehension barriers?
- Which topics require professional rather than automated guidance?
- What should not be inferred from population-level data about an individual user?

### 2. Ayurveda and Siddha usage

Investigate:

- Awareness, access, utilization, and reasons for use
- Practitioner availability and registration context
- Differences between Ayurveda and Siddha usage
- Tamil Nadu-specific Siddha context
- Use alongside conventional care
- Evidence limitations and safety concerns
- Quality, standardization, contamination, and interaction issues

UX perspective:

- How might existing patterns influence user expectations?
- What trust information may users need?
- Which assumptions about usage require interviews?
- Where could integrated-care journeys fail?

Do not treat prevalence of use, cultural importance, or official recognition as proof that a particular intervention is safe or effective.

### 3. Telemedicine adoption

Investigate:

- Adoption and use in India
- Urban and rural differences
- Device and connectivity constraints
- Preferred communication modes
- Trust, identity, privacy, payment, and continuity concerns
- Practitioner and patient responsibilities
- Situations unsuitable for remote consultation

UX perspective:

- Which users may be excluded?
- What information is needed before booking?
- How should the experience support failed connections and escalation to in-person care?
- Which metrics measure useful access rather than raw consultation volume?

### 4. User trust in traditional medicine

Investigate:

- Drivers of trust and distrust
- Roles of family, community, practitioners, government, online platforms, advertising, influencers, and testimonials
- Practitioner credentials and personal recommendations
- Perceptions of naturalness, safety, effectiveness, authenticity, and cultural familiarity
- Misinformation and exaggerated claims

UX perspective:

- Which trust signals should be researched with users?
- How might evidence and uncertainty be communicated without disrespecting culture?
- What could create false reassurance or authority?
- Which questions belong in interviews because published evidence is insufficient?

### 5. Food and nutrition behaviour

Investigate:

- Indian dietary patterns and diversity
- Regional and culturally familiar foods
- Vegetarian and non-vegetarian patterns
- Affordability, seasonality, household decision-making, and food access
- Salt, sugar, fat, fibre, protein, fruit, vegetable, pulse, millet, and whole-grain patterns where reliable data exists
- Food labels and processed-food behaviour
- Nutrition misinformation and food-as-medicine beliefs

UX perspective:

- What makes nutrition advice realistic or difficult to follow?
- Who makes food decisions in the household?
- Which recommendations can remain general education?
- Which conditions require a dietitian or clinician?
- How should Arogyam avoid blame, shame, or culturally inappropriate advice?

### 6. Medicine ordering and delivery

Investigate:

- User behaviour around online and offline medicine purchasing
- Prescription and non-prescription pathways
- Trust in sellers, brands, pharmacies, and marketplaces
- Authentication, substitution, stock, price, delivery, storage, returns, and support
- Ayurveda and Siddha medicine fulfilment
- Risks involving counterfeit, adulterated, contaminated, expired, or incorrectly dosed products
- Raw medicinal products, source identity, batch traceability, sustainability, and safe-use information

UX perspective:

- Which information is required before purchase?
- Where is pharmacist or practitioner oversight necessary?
- How should failures, substitutions, delays, and adverse events be handled?
- What user behaviours and concerns still require primary research?

### 7. Lab-test and health-record usage

Investigate:

- How users book and access diagnostic tests
- Accredited laboratory access
- Report receipt, storage, sharing, and loss
- Understanding of reference ranges and abnormal results
- Direct-to-consumer testing risks
- Digital health records, ABHA/ABDM awareness and use where reliable evidence exists
- Consent, caregiver access, interoperability, and privacy

UX perspective:

- What creates confusion or anxiety?
- How do users currently share reports with practitioners or caregivers?
- What controls and explanations are needed?
- What must remain under professional interpretation?

### 8. Digital and health literacy

Investigate:

- Ability to access, understand, evaluate, and act on health information
- Digital access, device sharing, connectivity, and assisted use
- Ability to recognize trustworthy sources, advertisements, and misinformation
- English, Tamil, medical terminology, readability, numeracy, and accessibility needs

UX perspective:

- What reading level, language, visual support, and interaction pattern may be appropriate?
- Who requires assisted journeys?
- How should comprehension be tested rather than assumed?
- Which groups may be hidden by national averages?

### 9. Rural and urban access differences

Investigate:

- Practitioner, facility, pharmacy, laboratory, delivery, transport, internet, and payment access
- Service availability and waiting time
- Affordability and travel burden
- Gender, age, disability, and household constraints
- State, district, and locality differences where reliable data exists

UX perspective:

- Which services must degrade gracefully or work at low bandwidth?
- What information depends on accurate local availability?
- Where might offline or assisted pathways be required?
- What should be researched separately by location?

### 10. Safety and regulatory requirements

Investigate the current Indian context for:

- Telemedicine
- Ayurveda and Siddha practitioner registration
- Professional and facility verification
- Health claims and advertising
- Ayurveda and Siddha medicines
- Ayurveda Aahara and applicable food requirements
- Pharmacy and medicine fulfilment where relevant
- Laboratory accreditation
- Consent, privacy, health records, and location data
- ABDM and ABHA integration
- Children’s information
- Medical-device implications if the product performs clinical interpretation or decision support

UX perspective:

- What information, consent, warning, verification, or user control is required?
- Which requirements affect the user journey?
- What needs formal Indian legal, regulatory, privacy, or clinical review?

Do not give definitive legal advice. Separate mandatory law, professional guidance, voluntary standards, and recommended product practice.

## Source Hierarchy

Prioritize original and authoritative sources:

1. Government of India and Government of Tamil Nadu publications and datasets
2. WHO, FAO, and other relevant United Nations health sources
3. ICMR and ICMR–National Institute of Nutrition
4. Ministry of Health and Family Welfare
5. Ministry of AYUSH, CCRAS, CCRS, and NCISM
6. National Health Authority and ABDM
7. FSSAI, CDSCO, NABL, and other applicable regulators or standards bodies
8. Large representative surveys such as NFHS and other official datasets
9. Cochrane reviews and peer-reviewed systematic reviews
10. Strong peer-reviewed primary studies
11. Reputable academic and professional bodies

Use commercial reports only for market estimates and disclose their methodology, date, access limitations, and possible conflicts. Do not use clinics, medicine sellers, supplement brands, influencers, or wellness blogs as evidence of medical effectiveness.

## Search Process

1. Define each research question before searching.
2. Specify geography, population, outcome, date range, and preferred source type.
3. Search authoritative domains and research indexes first.
4. Open and inspect the original document; do not rely on a search-result snippet.
5. Record the publication date, measured period, population, sample, method, and geography.
6. Check whether newer official evidence exists.
7. Compare multiple reliable sources for important or disputed claims.
8. Evaluate relevance to Arogyam rather than collecting unrelated statistics.
9. Record limitations, conflicts, and missing evidence.
10. Convert evidence gaps into primary-research questions.

## Evidence Appraisal

For every important source, assess:

- Authority and conflicts of interest
- Study or data-collection design
- Population and sample size
- Geographic relevance
- Measured period and publication date
- Outcome definition
- Bias and missing data
- Precision or uncertainty where reported
- Applicability to Arogyam users
- Whether the source supports description, association, or causation

Use these confidence labels:

- High confidence
- Moderate confidence
- Low confidence
- Conflicting evidence
- Not established
- Unable to verify

Do not assign high confidence solely because a source is official; assess what its data actually supports.

## Research Integrity Rules

- Never invent statistics, sources, studies, URLs, regulations, survey findings, or user behaviour.
- Every numerical or healthcare claim must have an inline citation and working direct link.
- Prefer the original document rather than a secondary summary.
- State the publication year and measured year or period.
- State population, geography, sample size, and method when available.
- If the full source cannot be inspected, do not use it as verified evidence.
- Separate fact, inference, interpretation, assumption, and recommendation.
- Do not generalize global evidence to India or national evidence to Tamil Nadu without discussing applicability.
- Do not convert association into causation.
- Do not treat traditional use or popularity as clinical proof.
- Do not infer individual diagnosis or treatment needs from population-level data.
- Do not invent user needs from statistics; use evidence to create questions for primary research.

## UX Synthesis Rules

For each verified finding, explain:

1. What the source establishes
2. Which population and context it covers
3. What it does not establish
4. Potential relevance to Arogyam
5. Possible UX implication
6. Assumption introduced by that implication
7. Interview or observation question needed to test the assumption

Example structure:

> **Evidence:** An authoritative source reports a measured pattern in a defined population.  
> **Limitation:** The evidence does not explain individual motivation or apply equally to all regions.  
> **UX implication:** Arogyam may need to account for a specific access or comprehension barrier.  
> **Primary-research question:** How do relevant users experience this barrier in their current care journey?

## Quality Gate

The secondary research is complete only when:

- All in-scope research areas have been searched or explicitly marked as evidence gaps.
- Important statistics and healthcare claims have direct authoritative citations.
- Publication dates and measured periods are not confused.
- National, Tamil Nadu, other-country, and global findings are clearly distinguished.
- Source limitations and applicability are visible.
- UX implications are framed as hypotheses rather than validated user needs.
- Primary-research questions are traceable to evidence gaps.
- No user interview finding has been invented.
