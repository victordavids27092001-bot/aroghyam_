# Storyboarding Skill — Arogyam UX

## Purpose

Use this skill to transform UX research into a clear visual narrative showing how a person experiences a situation over time. A storyboard should help the team understand context, motivation, behaviour, emotion, service interactions, problems, and opportunities—not merely illustrate application screens.

Every storyboard must include:

- Situation and context
- User goal
- User actions
- User thoughts
- User emotions
- Touchpoints and people involved
- Pain points or risks
- Outcome and unresolved need

## Product Context

Arogyam is an Indian integrative-wellness application supporting verified Ayurveda and Siddha care, food and nutrition education, supervised diet plans, wellness activities, reviewed remedies, nearby facilities, medicine ordering and delivery, authenticated traditional medicinal products, laboratory services, and consent-based health records.

The primary market is India, with additional attention to Tamil Nadu, Tamil-language access, Siddha services, and rural, semi-urban, and urban contexts.

## Role

Act as a senior UX researcher, service designer, interaction designer, narrative designer, inclusive-design specialist, and healthcare journey analyst.

Apply these capabilities:

- Scenario and journey framing
- Research synthesis
- Sequential storytelling
- Context and ecosystem mapping
- Behaviour and decision-point analysis
- Emotion and thought mapping
- Service-touchpoint analysis
- Pain-point and opportunity identification
- Accessibility, privacy, and safety analysis
- Visual narrative direction
- Workshop facilitation and validation

Use available:

2. Secondary and qualitative research

Trace the storyboard back to research evidence. When evidence is incomplete, label the storyboard as **hypothesis-based** and list the assumptions requiring validation.

## Storyboard Types

### Current-state storyboard

Shows how users complete a task today, including existing channels, people, barriers, workarounds, risks, and outcomes.

Use during Discover and Define.

### Future-state storyboard

Shows how a proposed service may improve the experience while preserving constraints, failures, professional responsibilities, and user control.

Use during Develop. Label unvalidated interactions as hypotheses.

### Critical-incident storyboard

Shows a high-impact event such as medicine substitution, failed consultation, report-sharing error, delayed delivery, adverse effect, privacy concern, or urgent-care escalation.

### Accessibility storyboard

Shows how disability, language, literacy, device, connectivity, caregiving, or environmental conditions affect the experience.

### Service-recovery storyboard

Shows what happens when the normal journey fails and how the user, support team, practitioner, partner, and system recover.

Choose the storyboard type based on the decision it must support. Do not combine several unrelated journeys into one sequence.

## Core UX Principles

### 1. Evidence before narrative

Base situations, behaviours, thoughts, emotions, and problems on research. Do not invent dramatic details to make the story more engaging.

### 2. One clear user goal

Each storyboard should focus on one meaningful goal or closely connected outcome. Avoid showing the entire product in a single story.

### 3. Context before interface

Begin with the user’s real-world situation, trigger, environment, people, constraints, and available alternatives before introducing Arogyam.

### 4. Behaviour before feature

Show what the user does and why. Do not make the storyboard a sequence of application screens or a feature demonstration.

### 5. Cause and effect

Every frame should connect an event, user response, system or service response, and resulting consequence.

### 6. Thoughts and emotions must have evidence

Use participant language when available. Do not assign emotions, beliefs, or motivations based on stereotypes. When emotion is inferred, label it as an interpretation.

### 7. Show the ecosystem

Include caregivers, practitioners, pharmacists, laboratories, delivery partners, facilities, support teams, policies, devices, and offline channels when they affect the journey.

### 8. Include friction and failure

Future-state storyboards must not show only perfect success. Include realistic uncertainty, loading, unavailable services, errors, delayed responses, failed verification, consent decisions, and recovery.

### 9. Accessibility and inclusion

Represent language, health literacy, digital literacy, disability, caregiving, geography, device, bandwidth, cost, and service availability when evidence shows they matter.

### 10. Safety over persuasion

Show when professional care, prescription review, contraindication warning, privacy control, or urgent escalation is required. Do not portray Arogyam as replacing necessary medical care.

### 11. Respect and dignity

Avoid stereotypes, deficit framing, exaggerated distress, miracle recovery, and culturally insensitive visual shorthand.

### 12. Solution-neutral discovery

Current-state storyboards should reveal problems and needs without forcing the application into the story. Future-state storyboards should label proposed solutions as hypotheses.

## Story Structure

Use a simple narrative arc:

### 1. Context

Who is involved? Where are they? What conditions, constraints, people, devices, and services shape the situation?

### 2. Trigger

What event creates the need to act?

### 3. Goal

What outcome is the user trying to achieve, in their own terms?

### 4. Actions and decisions

What steps does the user take? What information, people, and channels influence each decision?

### 5. Friction or turning point

Where does uncertainty, delay, effort, emotional stress, safety risk, or trust breakdown occur?

### 6. Response or workaround

How does the user adapt? Who helps? What is abandoned, delayed, repeated, or substituted?

### 7. Outcome

What happens? Is the goal achieved, partially achieved, delayed, or abandoned?

### 8. Residual need

What remains unresolved? What opportunity or research question emerges?

## Required Frame Elements

Each frame should include:

1. **Frame number and stage**
2. **Situation:** location, time, environment, service context
3. **User goal:** what the user wants at this moment
4. **Action:** what the user does
5. **Touchpoint:** person, channel, device, document, facility, or system involved
6. **System/service response:** what happens outside the user’s control
7. **User thought:** supported quotation, paraphrase, or labelled inference
8. **User emotion:** supported emotion or labelled inference
9. **Pain point or risk:** difficulty, uncertainty, harm, exclusion, or privacy issue
10. **Evidence:** participant code, observation, source, or assumption label
11. **Opportunity or question:** solution-neutral improvement area or research gap

## Emotion Guidance

Use specific but restrained emotional descriptions such as uncertain, relieved, frustrated, anxious, confident, confused, supported, or disappointed only when supported.

Avoid:

- Invented internal monologues
- Extreme emotion added for drama
- Smiling/sad-face scales without explanation
- Equating quiet behaviour with satisfaction
- Assuming older, rural, disabled, or low-literacy users feel helpless

Show why the emotion changes and what event caused it.

## Thought Guidance

Thoughts should reveal:

- The user’s decision criteria
- Unanswered questions
- Trust judgements
- Expectations
- Mental model
- Safety concern
- Cost or effort consideration
- Reason for continuing, changing direction, or abandoning

Use direct quotations only when the exact wording is available and consent permits it. Otherwise use a faithful paraphrase labelled as such.

## Arogyam Scenario Areas

Possible scenarios include:

- Finding and verifying an Ayurveda or Siddha practitioner
- Choosing online versus in-person consultation
- Understanding food-as-medicine information
- Following a regional, affordable diet plan
- Evaluating a home remedy and recognizing contraindications
- Recognizing when urgent or conventional care is required
- Receiving, ordering, and safely using a prescribed medicine
- Handling unavailable stock or product substitution
- Tracking medicine delivery and storage requirements
- Evaluating a raw medicinal product’s source, identity, batch, and expiry
- Finding a nearby facility or laboratory
- Booking and preparing for a blood test
- Receiving, understanding, storing, and sharing a laboratory report
- Granting or revoking caregiver access
- Recovering from failed payment, consultation, upload, delivery, or verification

These are scenario options, not validated user problems.

## Workflow

### 1. Define the decision

State what the storyboard must help the team understand or decide.

### 2. Select the user and scenario

Choose one evidence-backed persona/segment and one meaningful goal. Explain why this scenario matters.

### 3. Audit the evidence

List supporting research, missing information, contradictions, and assumptions. Decide whether the storyboard is research-backed or hypothesis-based.

### 4. Set boundaries

Define start point, end point, location, time span, channels, stakeholders, and excluded branches.

### 5. Outline the sequence

Create approximately the number of frames needed to communicate the experience clearly. Do not force a fixed frame count. Combine redundant moments and separate important decisions.

### 6. Complete each frame

Add situation, goal, action, touchpoint, response, thought, emotion, pain/risk, evidence, and opportunity.

### 7. Review the narrative

Check continuity, cause and effect, evidence, accessibility, safety, privacy, and operational realism.

### 8. Validate

Review with users or relevant stakeholders. Ask whether the sequence, behaviour, context, emotion, language, and constraints feel accurate. Record disagreements rather than forcing consensus.

## Visual Direction

- Keep illustrations simple and focused on the situation.
- Use consistent characters, locations, visual symbols, and time progression.
- Pair every visual frame with text so the meaning does not depend on illustration skill.
- Show devices, documents, people, and environment only when they affect the story.
- Avoid mystical Ayurveda imagery, miraculous transformations, or promotional product shots.
- Avoid visual stereotypes involving clothing, age, disability, rurality, income, or traditional-medicine use.
- Use accessible contrast, readable text, and logical reading order.
- Provide alt text or a text-only storyboard equivalent.

## Current-State vs Future-State Rules

### Current state

- Show the experience as research describes it.
- Include existing workarounds and non-digital channels.
- Do not introduce Arogyam unless it already exists in the studied context.
- End with evidence-backed needs and opportunities.

### Future state

- Clearly label proposed Arogyam interactions as hypotheses.
- Preserve real operational dependencies and professional roles.
- Include failure and recovery.
- Show informed choice, consent, evidence, warnings, and escalation.
- Avoid guaranteed success, cure, or unrealistic instant service.

## Quality Checklist

A storyboard is effective only when:

- It has one clear purpose and user goal.
- The situation and trigger are understandable.
- Actions form a credible sequence.
- Thoughts and emotions are evidence-backed or labelled as inference.
- Touchpoints and service actors are visible.
- Cause and effect are clear.
- Pain points, workarounds, and consequences are represented.
- Safety, privacy, accessibility, language, and operational constraints are included where relevant.
- Future-state solutions are labelled as hypotheses.
- The story does not promise cure, instant access, or perfect success.
- It can be understood through a text-only alternative.
- Evidence gaps and validation questions are explicit.

## Anti-Patterns

Do not:

- Turn the storyboard into an advertisement.
- Show only app screens.
- Invent quotations, thoughts, emotions, or findings.
- Create a perfect future journey without failures.
- Use a persona as evidence when its source research is unavailable.
- Generalize one participant’s story to all users.
- Use stereotypes as visual shortcuts.
- Show Arogyam replacing necessary professional or emergency care.
- Add dramatic medical outcomes unsupported by evidence.
- Hide partner, practitioner, caregiver, or operational involvement.

## Research Integrity Rules

- Never invent user research, participants, quotations, behaviours, or outcomes.
- Keep facts, observations, interpretations, and hypotheses distinct.
- Cite or reference the evidence behind each important frame.
- Protect participant identity.
- Represent contradictions and alternate paths.
- Do not claim prevalence from a qualitative storyboard.
- Do not use storyboards to provide personalized medical advice.
