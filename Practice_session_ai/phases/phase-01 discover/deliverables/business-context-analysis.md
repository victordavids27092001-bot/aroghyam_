# Arogyam Business Context Analysis

## 1. Executive Summary

Arogyam is proposed as a bridge between culturally familiar Ayurveda and Siddha wellbeing practices, evidence-based nutrition, verified professional care, and responsible digital-health services in India. Tamil Nadu is the initial regional focus.

The BRD describes a broad ecosystem rather than a single application feature: education, practitioner discovery, consultations, food guidance, wellness activities, remedies, medicines and raw medicinal products, laboratories, records, facilities, caregiver access, community participation, and emergency escalation.

At this stage, these are project intentions and value hypotheses. The supplied material does not yet establish user demand, partner availability, operational readiness, commercial viability, or the effectiveness of individual interventions. Discover must test the problem and trust assumptions before Define selects an MVP.

The highest-risk areas are medical claims, delayed appropriate care, practitioner and seller verification, medicine quality and interactions, laboratory interpretation, health-data privacy, community misinformation, commercial conflicts, and exclusion caused by language, literacy, disability, geography, device, connectivity, or cost.

## 2. Why Arogyam Is Being Created

### Project-stated purpose

The BRD proposes that Indian users seeking preventive care and traditional wellbeing face fragmented services, inconsistent verification, exaggerated claims, unsafe self-medication, uncertain product quality, and confusion about appropriate medical escalation.

### Intended experience change

The intended shift is from disconnected and difficult-to-verify services toward a coordinated experience containing evidence labels, verified professionals and partners, culturally relevant nutrition, continuity of records and follow-up, and visible safety boundaries.

### What remains unverified

- How target users currently experience the stated fragmentation
- Which problems are frequent, severe, and worth solving first
- Whether users want one integrated service or prefer specialist services
- Which offline trust relationships can transfer online
- Which users will adopt online, assisted, or mixed-channel care
- Whether the proposed services are operationally and commercially feasible

## 5. Target Geography

### Project-stated scope

- Primary market: India
- Additional focus: Tamil Nadu
- Initial languages: English and Tamil
- Intended contexts: urban, semi-urban, and rural

### UX implications to investigate

- Siddha awareness, practitioner supply, and facility discovery in Tamil Nadu
- Regional food terminology, household preparation, affordability, and seasonality
- English/Tamil code-switching and medical-term comprehension
- Address quality, transport, delivery, maps, payments, device access, and connectivity
- Trust in government, practitioners, family referral, pharmacies, laboratories, and digital platforms
- Need for telephone, caregiver, clinic-assisted, or offline continuation

An initial Tamil Nadu launch is a project direction, not yet a validated market or operational decision.

## 11. UX Implications

- Trust must be designed through verifiable identity, evidence, transparency, dependable service, and recovery—not aesthetic tradition alone.
- Arogyam is a multi-actor service; front-end convenience will fail without credential, pharmacy, laboratory, delivery, content, moderation, and incident operations.
- Food-as-medicine requires careful language testing because supportive nutrition can easily be interpreted as curative treatment.
- Offline and assisted pathways are part of the service, particularly for trust transfer, older adults, caregivers, low digital literacy, and poor connectivity.
- Safety-critical information must remain visible across consultation, remedy, medicine, laboratory, report, record, and community journeys.
- The proposed breadth creates major scope risk. MVP prioritization must wait for problem, supply, governance, and feasibility evidence.

## 12. Priority Discover Questions

1. What event leads a person to seek Ayurveda, Siddha, nutrition, a remedy, a test, or conventional care?
2. How do users decide whom and what to trust, and how do they verify those decisions?
3. What does food-as-medicine mean to different users, and where does it create unsafe expectations?
4. Which offline relationships and artifacts should continue online?
5. What prevents consultation, follow-up, diet, activity, medicine, or test recommendations from fitting daily life?
6. How do users currently obtain traditional medicines and raw products, and what quality failures do they encounter?
7. How are laboratory tests selected and reports stored, explained, and shared?
8. When do users self-treat or delay conventional care, and what would change that decision safely?
9. Which language, literacy, disability, cost, device, connectivity, transport, and family factors affect access?
10. Which proposed services create enough value to justify ongoing use or payment?
